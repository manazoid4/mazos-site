## Post 1 — post first

For three days my own contact form was broken and I had no idea.

I'd moved my site to a new domain. The form still looked fine. It still said "thanks, message sent." But the email service had quietly stopped accepting submissions from the new address — it only trusted the old one.

I found it by filling in my own form with test data from both addresses and comparing what came back. Not by reading the code. The code was fine.

I don't know how many enquiries I lost in those three days. I never will.

If you've changed your domain, your host, your booking tool or your email address in the last year: go and send yourself a test enquiry right now. Use a real email. Wait for it to actually arrive.

I only found mine because I went looking.

I build websites, automation, software and small physical products for businesses. Mostly what I do is find the thing that's quietly costing you money.

#SmallBusiness #WebDesign

## Post 3

The smallest text on my own website was 9 pixels tall.

Not a typo. Nine. On a phone. I'd been sending people to that site for months.

I only found it because I measured instead of looked. Opened it in a real browser at phone size and asked what the actual rendered size was, in pixels, for every piece of text on the page.

It looked fine to me. Of course it did. I knew what it said.

That's the thing about the problems worth fixing. They're not broken, so nothing alerts you. They're just quietly worse than you think, and you've stopped being able to see them because you're the one who built it.

It's fixed now, across the whole site. I don't know what it cost me before — nobody measured that, so I'm not going to pretend I know.

Try it on yours. Open your site on your phone, at arm's length, in daylight. Can you read your prices?

#WebDesign #SmallBusiness

## Post 4

Public sector contracts get published constantly. Technically they're all available to anyone.

They're also spread across dozens of portals, tagged badly, and mostly irrelevant to any one firm. So in a lot of trades businesses somebody sits down and scrolls through them by hand, weekly, to find the few worth bidding on.

So I built JobFilter. It finds published contracts, checks them against what a firm actually does, and throws the rest away.

What it does not do is win you the work. It can't. The bid is still yours and so is the decision at the other end. Anyone whose tool promises you contract awards is selling you something else.

It's live at jobfilter.uk with paid plans. It's my own product, not a client job.

This is what I mean by automation. Not a transformation programme. One repetitive job, done properly, by software.
